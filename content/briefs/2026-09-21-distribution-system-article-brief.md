## What you're building

A mega-resource X article. The model is "How to become a Robotics Engineer in 6 months (RESOURCES)", which I pasted in full in the session on 21 Sep. Go read it before you start writing, because the format is the whole point.

What makes that piece work, and what you have to copy:

1. Every section ends with something the reader physically owns. A file, a sheet, a published piece. Never "now you understand X".
2. Every resource has a real link, and every price was checked against the vendor.
3. Where a widely-quoted number didn't survive checking, the author says so instead of repeating it.
4. It tells the reader where people normally start and why that start fails.
5. Milestones per phase, written as things the reader can now do.
6. One honest ask near the top, then the article.

What we are not copying: it's a "become a robotics engineer" piece for someone starting a career. Ours is for an operator who already runs an agency. They keep the identity they have. They leave with a machine running.

## The angle

This is the section that makes the piece mine, and it goes near the top, right after the promise.

Everyone is doing AI content now. So the thing worth doing is AI content that doesn't read as AI. You use AI to sound more natural and more original, not less.

My own method is the proof. I talk into voice notes and the writing gets built off my actual voice, which is why it doesn't sound like a tool wrote it. That's the mechanism, and the reader can copy it the same day.

The same point on subagents: a subagent is good at collecting ideas and raw notes. The effort has to go into displaying them the way the algorithm rewards. Right now X articles are working really well, and the effort shows up in the format.

## The roadmap

Five phases, mapped across the 90 days. Every phase gets resources with real links, a focus list, and a practical task that leaves the reader holding something.

### Phase 1: build the brain

The first task costs nothing, and it's the one nobody does.

Collect everything into one place. Every call transcription, all your client work, all your case studies, all your wins, your wins channel if you have one. All of it. Then build a voice document off your own recordings, not off your existing posts, because posts are already edited and voice notes aren't.

Practical task: the folder, then the voice doc. Zero dollars.

Reference `skills/research/voice-extraction.md` for the method. Don't paste the skill file into the article, give them the structure.

### Phase 2: pick one channel

If you want X, do X. Don't run X and LinkedIn at the same time.

Practical task: write down the channel, and write down the one you're deliberately not doing yet.

### Phase 3: choose and track the inputs, weekly

Comments, replies, DMs sent, articles published. Weekly numbers, tracked in one sheet.

Comments on very targeted profiles and DMs both work from day zero, before any content exists, and they grow the account on their own.

The numbers have to fit the hours. I protect 5 to 7 hours a week on this brand, about an hour a day, so the targets in the article need to be survivable at that level. Juan: leave these as brackets in the draft and I'll set them.

Practical task: four straight weeks of tracked inputs in one sheet.

### Phase 4: find the competitors doing what you want to do

Practical task: five accounts, and what specifically you're taking from each one.

### Phase 5: split the output by vehicle

Giveaways, articles, short-form tweets. Each one is its own lane with its own job.

Use `skills/content/vehicle-library.md` for the vehicle split.

Practical task: ship the same source material as three different vehicles without repeating yourself.

## Milestones

Written as what the reader can do. Copy this structure from the robotics piece, one block at the end of each month.

**End of month 1**
- Open one place and find the transcript of any call you've had, every case study, every win, and the client work worth showing
- Pull three publishable angles out of that brain without writing anything from scratch
- Say which channel you're on, and name the one you're deliberately not doing yet
- Hand your brain to Claude and get something back that sounds like you instead of like a tool

**End of month 2**
- Hit your weekly input numbers without deciding what to do each morning
- Name five accounts doing what you want to do, and say what you're taking from each
- Ship the same source as three different vehicles without repeating yourself
- Format an article properly, where cover, title and first three lines are built as one unit

**End of month 3**
- Say which piece produced which conversation, and be right
- Read four weeks of inputs and tell which lane is producing replies from the right people
- Run the whole week inside the hours you actually have, without it eating delivery
- Point at a booked call and trace it back to a specific post, article or DM

## The claims that don't survive checking

This section is mandatory. Source is my own thread, `research/transcripts/maurojpelle/2026-09-04-x-source-code-myth-buster-thread.md`.

The X For You ranking code is open source. I asked Claude to read it.

- "A reply is worth 13.5 likes" is a 2023 number, from a system that got replaced.
- "A repost is 20x" is a 2023 number.
- "One reply beats 150 likes" is a 2023 number.
- "Links get suppressed 80%" has no basis in the published code. There is no link filter, and nothing in the scorer touches external links.
- "Posting more stacks" is wrong. There's a diversity function that decays your own posts against each other inside one feed response. Your best post keeps its value and the weaker ones absorb the decay. Five posts don't buy five slots in one person's feed.

The part I won't overclaim, and this stays in: the code gives you the formula and the full list of what's scored, and zero of the weight values. So anyone telling you what a repost is worth in 2026 is guessing. Me included.

Also worth keeping: a like is not the thing to optimise. Profile clicks, follows, DM shares, copy-link shares, dwell time and quoted clicks are all in the scorer. A post that earns a profile visit and a follow is working more of it than one that earns a like.

## Voice

Read `brand/voice.md` in full before writing a line, and run the pre-publish checklist before calling it done. The bans that get broken most:

1. No em dashes. Ever.
2. No "it's not X, it's Y" and no variant of it.
3. No "most brands" or "most people" openers.
4. No trailing summary sentences. End on the point.
5. No problem-to-purpose reversals.
6. Never invent a number. Bracket it.

Register: plain direct English, short declaratives, one idea per line. Operator talking to a smart peer. Hand over the real prompts. Leave the odd casual aside in. Call out the reader's likely mistake once, mid-article.
