# Article corpus: finding and saving outlier articles

The older process, and the one `profile-study.md` is modelled on. Source: Mauro's loom of
2026-08-28, transcript at `research/transcripts/maurojpelle/2026-08-28-how-to-save-outlier-x-articles-loom.md`,
plus what the first seven captures exposed.

**Where the corpus lives.** Covers and craft captures in `research/outlier-x-articles/`. The
measured corpus, 41 captures with metrics, is `~/growthub-os/research/article-studies/`, which is
account-agnostic and serves both repos.

**The job.** Find X articles that clearly outperformed, save them whole, and build nothing until
there are enough of them to draw a rule from.

---

## The one rule that makes this work

**Save only. Do not build.**

No skill gets written, no template gets made, no pattern gets hardened, until roughly 50 articles
are in. Save, save, save. That is the whole instruction for the collection phase.

The reason is simple. A rule drawn from five articles is a rule drawn from what you happened to
like that week.

---

## Finding outliers

**X gives you no way to filter by views.** You cannot sort an account's articles by performance,
so you go looking by hand.

Two routes, and use both:

**Start from accounts you already rate.** Open the profile, go to their articles, and read across
them. Articles show a view count, which is why this works at all. Regular posts no longer expose
theirs, so articles are the only place an outlier is visible without an export.

**Then browse Home for large accounts you do not follow.** A big follower count is a reason to
look, not a reason to save. Plenty of large accounts publish articles that are not worth capturing.

## The quality bar, and the tell that fails it

The most common failure is an article that is a YouTube script pasted into X.

It is obvious once you know to look. The structure is spoken, not written, and it reads as a
transcript with headers. Do not save those, whatever the account's follower count is.

What you are looking for is an article someone actually built for the format.

## The four components, plus the number

Per article, five things:

**1. Title screenshot.** Taken **from the feed, not from inside the article.** This is the part
people get wrong. Shot from the feed, the frame carries the title, the cover image and the opening
lines of the text in one image. From inside, you lose the cover and the framing.

**2. The cover image.** If the cover is a plain screenshot, the title screenshot already has it and
you do not need a second file.

If the cover is an HTML infographic or anything with fine detail, do not screenshot it. Right
click, copy image. The detail survives that way and does not survive a screenshot.

**3. The full text** of the article.

**4. The link** to the post.

**5. The view count.** Views are the only number used to decide what counts as an outlier, so an
article captured without its views cannot be read as one later.

## Handing them over

Batch them. Do not send one at a time.

Save them to a bookmarks folder as you go, then hand the batch over in chat with all five
components per article.

Each one becomes a numbered file in the corpus folder, with its cover stored alongside it and a
row added to the index table.

## What the first seven captures got wrong

Worth reading before you start, because these are cheap to avoid and expensive to fix later.

**Only one of five rows had a view count.** Which means the corpus was selecting on Mauro liking
the craft rather than on measured performance. Fine for a library of cover ideas. Not fine for
drawing any rule about reach.

**Three of seven were cover and title only,** with no body text and no views. They cannot be read
as outliers at all and had to be moved into a separate cover library.

**Links were missing on almost all of them,** and a link would have fixed both problems in one
pass, because everything else can be re-fetched from it.

**So: the link and the view count are not optional fields.** They are what makes a capture a
capture rather than a screenshot you liked.

**Tag the author on every row.** Different authors means there is no house style averaging out
across the pile. Without the author column you cannot later check whether a pattern is real or is
one account's habit.

---

## Reading the corpus, once it is full

**Log the readings. Do not harden them into rules.**

An observation across seven captures is a note. The same observation across fifty, holding per
author, is a rule.

One live example of the difference: not one cover in the first seven is a raw screenshot. Four of
five are illustrations and the fifth arranges real screenshots into a designed composite. That runs
against the proof-of-work-screenshot convention recorded elsewhere, and it is currently a tension,
not a finding.

---

## Why this is worth doing at all

Same reason as the profile study. A breakdown of other people's work borrows their authority, and
it comes in two forms.

The name: "I broke down @author's article."

The number: "I broke down 40 articles."

The number form is why the corpus is built to 50 rather than to 5.
