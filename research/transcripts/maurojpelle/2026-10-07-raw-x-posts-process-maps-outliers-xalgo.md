### A2

Our first process map was boxes in a straight line, one under the other, with arrows between them. Every step in there, order right.

It's just a long paragraph with arrows.

What fixed it was asking why they all have to be on a straight line. When claude does consecutive steps there's no reason to draw them as separate boxes stacked in the same column as the human ones.

So now:

* every run of consecutive claude steps goes into one orange block off to the side
* mini blocks inside it name the skills being used
* human steps stay as single boxes in the column

"find the proven post" gets "mostly 300 plus comments on linkedin". "capture it" gets "copy the text, screenshot the comment or reply, paste the image into claude".

Draw it that way and you can see at a glance where a person is actually needed and where nobody is.

### A4

Spent half the morning on a question that sounds stupid until you try to answer it. What counts as a process?

A linkedin auto dm is one part of our linkedin strategy. So is that a process? Is linkedin a process?

Too narrow and you're drawing maps of individual tasks nobody opens. Too broad and you've drawn a channel and learned nothing from it.

Where we landed, a process has:

* one trigger, something arrives, a proven post, a brief, a monday
* one output that ships on its own, a post, an article, a board
* one person who presses go
* one number it moves on monday
* between 6 and 12 steps

"make the image" fails it, nothing ships on its own, so it's a step inside something else. "linkedin strategy" fails it the other way, no single output, so it's a channel, which is really just a set of processes with a weekly quota on top.

The steps every process runs, the draft check, the staging page, the person who schedules it, get drawn once as shared modules instead of copy pasted into every map.

### A5

A lot of the boxes on my process map have a question mark where the time should be.

Leaving them.

Every box gets a real number the first time that process runs on the clock, and not before. I'm not filling them in from memory and I'm not letting claude estimate them either.

The number sitting on top of the whole map is booked calls per hour of human time, and that only works if the hours are real:

* a process shipping three weeks with no calls is a candidate to retire
* a process booking calls with almost none of my time gets more volume

A map full of confident numbers nobody measured is worse than a map with gaps in it.

## Batch B: outlier capture and templates

### B1

How I capture an outlier post so it's actually usable later.

Same five things every time, no exceptions:

* copy the post text
* screenshot the post from the feed, not from inside the article
* copy the link
* check the numbers are precise before any of it moves
* batch them, never one at a time

The feed screenshot matters more than it sounds. You want the title, the cover and the first few lines in one frame, because that's the unit that did the work.

If the cover is an infographic I copy the image instead of screenshotting it, so the detail inside survives the capture.

The link and the view count are the two I won't skip. With a link everything else can be re-fetched. Without one you've saved a screenshot of something you'll never find again.

### B2

A post with 100,000 views isn't an outlier. It might just be a Tuesday for that account.

An outlier is a post that did way better than that account normally does, which means you compare it to their own feed and never to yours.

How we score it:

* take the post's number, impressions on x, comments on linkedin
* divide it by that account's median over its last 10 to 20 posts
* 3x or more is an outlier
* 10x is a strong one

With a floor underneath so a tiny account doesn't win on ratio alone. At least 50,000 impressions on x, at least 300 comments on linkedin.

Follower count tells you nothing here. I looked at an account with 11.5k followers once and every article was a YouTube script pasted into x, headers and all. Nothing on it worth saving.

### B4

When we started collecting outlier articles the instruction was to save them and not touch a single skill until we had about 50.

Not three. Not ten. About 50.

I don't want claude writing skills after three examples, because:

* a rule drawn from five posts is a rule drawn from what you liked that week
* the same observation across fifty, held per author, is a rule
* everything in between is a note

Save, save, save. Then look at what the winners have in common and decide whether any of it is worth a template.

The collection is still mid-way and I haven't written the skill yet.

### B5

Our post templates live in a doc, not inside the claude skills. That split is deliberate.

What goes in the doc:

* the shape, with the slots marked
* the rules that made it work
* the original, with its real numbers next to it

What goes in the skill:

* the writing rules
* the bans
* how to check a claim

Formats change constantly. Bake a template into a skill and you're rewriting skills every week. The skill holds the stuff that doesn't move.

Every Monday we look at which templates won. One that won runs again with a new subject. One that lost twice is retired, because the hub only stays useful if things leave it.

## Batch C: reading the x ranking file

### C1

There's no 10 second rule in the x algorithm.

A post went round this week saying the time after a tap only counts from 10 seconds.

I went through param.rs looking for it. There's no threshold in there at all:

* the model predicts dwell time as a value
* no floor, no cutoff, no 10
* four separate dwell params and none of them gate on seconds

Treat the 10 seconds as that author's reading, not as something x published.

### C2

Share via copy link is worth 20 in the x scoring file. It's the highest positive weight in there and nobody talks about it.

For comparison, from the same file:

* share via copy link, 20
* reply, 5
* quote, 5
* retweet, 1
* like, 0.5
* open link, 0.2

A like is worth a fortieth of someone sending your post to one person in a dm.

It makes sense when you look at what the action is. It's private, it's deliberate, and nobody does it to perform. Which is exactly why the ranker works hardest to predict it.

Write things people forward to one person, not things people applaud in public.

**Correction logged 2026-10-07:** `ShareViaCopyLink` is not a dm send, and the "a fortieth" division is the
counts misreading `brand/claims.md` names directly (the weights multiply predicted probabilities). Both lines
are fixed in the LinkedIn version. The rest of the post is his.

### C3

Checked the x algorithm post that went round this week, line by line against the code. Part one, what it got right.

All four of these are in param.rs today:

* the tap on a post, 0.3
* the time spent after the tap, 0.4
* not interested, -47.52
* insult-labelled posts hidden from non-followers

The second one is the one that changes how you write. Time after the tap is worth more than the tap.

A hook that wins the click and loses the reader now scores below one that holds them to the end.

Careful with that number though. There are four dwell params in the file. 0.05 is dwell with no tap, 0.004 is another, and 0.4 is specifically time after a click. Quote the wrong one and the advice inverts.

### C4

The fresh post pool got switched on on 30 Sep. A post from the day before said it was still off.

What it covers now, straight from the cold start params:

* accounts under 50,000 followers
* posts under 200 impressions
* in their first 2 hours
* dropped into feed slots 15 and 16

If you're under 50k that two hour window is the whole game. Post when your audience is actually online and answer every reply inside it.

One thing worth saying. Every number in that file is a default, and x can override any of them per experiment or per user without shipping a release.

### C5

Posting five times doesn't buy you five slots in one person's feed. That hasn't changed with this release and it's still the thing most people get wrong.

There's a diversity function that decays your own posts against each other inside a single feed response:

* your best post keeps its full value
* the weaker ones absorb the decay
* it never drops to zero
* and it sorts by score, not by time

So the posts eating the penalty are the ones you cared least about.

You are basically competing with yourself at some times.
