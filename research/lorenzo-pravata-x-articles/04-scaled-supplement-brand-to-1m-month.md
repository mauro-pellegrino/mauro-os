## Article body

A supplement brand we manage just hit $1M in a single month on Meta ads.

Supplements is one of the hardest niches to scale right now.

You compete against VC-funded brands that can stomach a $200 CPA, CPMs are brutal, Meta's policy team cuts half your claims before you start, and every ad in the feed looks the same.

It's also one of the few DTC niches where you can realistically run to $10M a month and beyond.

Last year alone we managed $17M in supplement spend on Meta, with several clients at $1M/month and a few that reached $50M and $100M a year. Every one of those accounts scaled on the same 4 levers.

I'll go through them in order, because the order matters.

### Lever 1. Positioning

Almost every stuck supplement account we audit has a creative problem on the surface and a positioning problem underneath.

There are only a few real moves.

1. Niche down
2. Target a new avatar
3. Name a new mechanism
4. Or layer two of those together.

Primal Queen took a category that was being sold to men and rebuilt it for women.

Norse Organics ran a new avatar on the same product.

Arrae niched all the way down to women with chronic digestive discomfort and named a mechanism for it, which is why their ads read like a diagnosis instead of a pitch.

One warning from experience.

You can't A/B test positioning the way you A/B test ads. Commit to the move first, then test angles, formats and funnels under it.

Brands that try to split-test their identity end up with ads that say nothing.

### Lever 2. Creatives

The creative game in supplements (and in most niches) is win rate multiplied by volume, per buyer persona.

5-10 new concepts a week is a very good volume to start with. Andromeda killed the single-hero-ad approach, so those concepts have to be visually different from each other, different formats and settings and faces, not ten re-scripts of the same UGC template.

What's winning for our supplement clients right now, in the feed today:

- Podcast ads that open with a mechanism diagnosis (naming the real reason the problem exists)
- Pharmacist ads, in-studio, white-coat authority
- Street interviews with an authority figure ("I'm a nutrition researcher")
- Educational ads at a whiteboard or in a supermarket aisle
- Comparison ads against a named alternative, Ozempic being the obvious one
- One-shot personal experience videos from a creator

None of these open like an ad. That's the point. Cold traffic in this niche has seen every testimonial format a thousand times.

### Lever 3. Offer, CRO and AOV

The math has to work before the creative can. Cost of delivery, meaning COGS plus shipping, needs to sit below 20-25% or the account never gets room to scale.

From there it's bundles, volume discounts, post-purchase upsells and free shipping thresholds. And the funnel has to match awareness.

A simple PDP for solution-aware traffic, listicles for problem-aware, advertorials for problem-aware audiences over 45, and a quiz funnel when you want to open up unaware audiences and a bigger TAM.

Older audiences will sit through a VSL after the quiz.

Younger ones want a customized sales page.

### Lever 4. Subscription and LTV

This is the lever that decides whether $1M/month is a peak or a baseline.

Subscription opt-in should run 80% or higher. The best brands we've seen hit 95%+. Free gifts at month 2 and month 3 keep people in long enough for the habit to form, and the habit is the retention.

Past $500K/month, 90-day LTV becomes the number to watch (not ROAS, which stops telling the truth at that scale).

If 90-day LTV holds while you scale, keep pushing spend. If it slides, the problem is upstream in one of the first three levers.

Work the levers in order. Positioning first, creatives on top of it, offer math under both, subscription to keep what you paid for.

## Structural skeleton (the part that ports)

1. **Result in line one, difficulty in line two.** The $1M lands immediately, then the article immediately argues the niche is hard. Difficulty framing protects the number from reading as a brag and makes it a proof of method instead.
2. **Zoom out from one case to the pattern.** "Last year alone we managed $17M in supplement spend... every one of those accounts scaled on the same 4 levers." One result becomes evidence of a repeatable system in two sentences. This is belief 1 (systems beat random effort) executed cleanly.
3. **"I'll go through them in order, because the order matters."** A sequencing promise. It tells the reader the list is a diagnostic, not a menu, which is what makes them read all four instead of skimming.
4. **Named third-party brands as examples** (Primal Queen, Norse Organics, Arrae). Public brands, not clients, so nothing is disclosed. Each one illustrates a different positioning move in a single line.
5. **A warning from experience** inside lever 1 ("you can't A/B test positioning"). One hard-won rule per section makes the section feel earned rather than researched.
6. **Escalating specificity by lever:** positioning is conceptual, creatives is a list, offer is arithmetic (20-25% COGS), subscription is a threshold (80%, 95%, 90-day LTV past $500K/month). The article gets more concrete as it goes, which is the opposite of how most people write.
7. **A diagnostic close, not a summary.** "If 90-day LTV holds, keep pushing. If it slides, the problem is upstream." The reader leaves with a decision rule.
8. **CTA phrased as a diagnosis, not a pitch:** "Want me to look at which lever is capping your account?" The article created the frame (four levers, one is capping you), then the CTA offers to apply it.

**The strongest structural idea in this one:** the article installs a diagnostic frame the reader can't unsee, then sells the diagnosis. The CTA only works because the four levers exist. That's a more sophisticated close than 01, 02 or 03.
