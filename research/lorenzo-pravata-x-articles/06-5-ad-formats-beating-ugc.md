## Article body

We produce over 500 ads a month across every format we run.

The ones that win keep sharing the same trait. They don't look like ads.

Here are the 5 formats beating standard UGC for us right now, and why each one works.

### Podcast-style ads

A host talks to camera the way they would on a real show. No pitch in the first frame, just a conversation that happens to land on a product.

It works because the viewer doesn't brace. Standard UGC signals you're being sold to in the first second, a person holding the product, the studio lighting, the script. People are numb to it.

A podcast clip reads as content, so the first 20 seconds buy you attention before the sell starts. We build the setup, the framing, the two-person dynamic so it looks like a real episode, not an ad wearing a podcast costume.

The trap is making it look like an ad with a podcast skin. The mic and the backdrop aren't the format. The conversation is. If the host pivots into ad-speak at second 3, you've lost the whole advantage.

### Skit conversations

Two people acting out a short scene, a quick exchange that feels like a moment you've actually had, and the product slides into the middle of it.

It works because the viewer watches for the story, not the pitch. A skit dramatizes the problem and the objection through dialogue. One person doubts, the other answers, and the audience sees their own hesitation handled on screen without being lectured. It plays like entertainment, so people finish it.

And a finished view is where the selling happens.

The trap is writing it like an ad with two actors. The dialogue has to sound like how people actually talk, and the doubt has to be a real doubt. If both characters are reading benefits at each other, it's a testimonial in a wig.

### Founder story, straight to camera

One person, talking directly, telling you why they built the thing.

Trust transfers from a face faster than from a logo. A founder who names the exact problem they had, in their own language, reads as real in a way a brand account never will.

The key is specificity. Not "I wanted to make a better product." The exact frustration, the exact moment, the detail only someone who lived it would mention.

This is also the cheapest winning format to produce. One person, one camera, a real story. No studio.

### The confession / green-screen offer

Someone opens with a confession. "I need to apologize to anyone who bought this, because I lied."

Then they walk it back. The product is real, the results are real, the only lie is that it's cheaper now. New viewers stay because the hook breaks the pattern, and by the time the offer lands they've already been sold. Dollar Shave Club and True Classic both run versions of this. It works as a green screen over good visuals or just a voiceover.

It earns cold and warm audiences at once. You communicate an offer and explain the product in the same 60 seconds, instead of choosing one.

### Native statics and trending AI formats

Not everything that scales is video. Native-looking statics, the kind that read like a screenshot or a meme instead of a designed ad, are quietly carrying a lot of spend. Lately Pixar-style and AI-animated ads are working too.

The reason is the same one running under all of this. What works in organic ends up working in ads, and the ads that work don't look like ads. A native static doesn't announce itself in the feed.

By the time the brain registers it's an ad, it's already been read.

These move fast though. A trending format is a window, not a permanent edge. The Pixar ads work now because it's new.

Pick any of these apart and the same mechanism sits underneath. The viewer doesn't realize it's an ad until they're already in.

## Structural skeleton (the part that ports)

1. **Production-volume proof in line one** (500 ads a month), then the finding that volume produced. The number isn't a brag, it's the sample size that licenses the claim.
2. **A named enemy in the title.** `beating ugc` gives the reader a side to be on. The other five articles name a competitor, a tool, a result or a question. This one names a thing the reader is currently doing that isn't working.
3. **Identical internal template per format, five times:**
   - what it is, one sentence
   - **why it works**, mechanism-level
   - **the trap**, the way people execute it badly
   The repetition is the readability. You can drop into any section and get the same three things.
4. **"The trap" is the highest-value slot in each section.** Naming the failure mode is what separates this from a listicle. It's also what makes it hard to copy, because the trap is only knowable from having run the format.
5. **Third-party proof for the format nobody would believe** (Dollar Shave Club, True Classic on the confession format). Public brands, no client disclosure.
6. **One honest limitation** (trending formats are a window, not a permanent edge), which buys credibility for the other four.
7. **A through-line section that collapses all five into one mechanism**, then restates the volume × win rate thesis.
8. **CTA conditioned on the reader's symptom:** "if your UGC has stopped pulling". Same DM-keyword-as-diagnosis pattern as 02, 03, 04, 05.

**Why this one travelled.** A list of five with a named enemy is the most forwardable shape in the set. Five thumbnails on the card promise five things visually before anyone reads a word. And 23 replies suggests it invited disagreement, which the other five didn't. A stance about what's *beating* something else gives people a reason to argue.
