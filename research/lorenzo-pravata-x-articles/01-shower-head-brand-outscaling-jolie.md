## Article body

A filtered shower head brand did $40M last year and owns the category.

There's a competitor most people have never heard of that now pulls more monthly traffic, and probably more new customers, than the leader does.

We manage over $100M a year in Meta spend, so here's exactly how the challenger scaled in a market it didn't invent, the full-funnel-in-one-ad structure they use, and the four gaps I'd fix if I took the account over tomorrow.

### First, why their tactics work here

You can't run the same playbook in every category.

What works depends on how saturated the market already is.

There are five stages of market sophistication:

1. First to market. You just name the benefit. "Filter your shower."
2. Competitors arrive. You make a bigger claim. "Filters 99% of contaminants."
3. Claims start to numb people. You lead with a mechanism. "Three-action filtration."
4. The mechanism gets copied. You need a better or more specific one.
5. The audience has heard everything. You win on identity and experience, not louder claims.

Filtered shower heads became a category in 2021.

It's now late stage three moving into stage four.

The plain claim is burned, and the leader owns stages one and two. So the challenger couldn't win by explaining the same mechanism louder.

They had to earn attention a different way. Here's how:

### They sell through an advertorial, not the ad

This is pretty simple to understand, because advertorials aren't new.

They run one hero product at $100, with a volume discount ($150 for two, $200 for three) and a filter subscription upsell.

That higher average order value matters more than it looks.

The more profit you pull from each order, the more you can afford to spend to acquire a customer.

That's how a brand goes from spending $10k a month to $100k or more, and on Meta the brand that can bid more usually wins the customer.

63% of their ads point to an advertorial-style landing page, not the product page.

The ad doesn't have to do all the selling. It hooks, then hands off to long-form content that educates and closes.

In a category where buyers are already in research mode, that advertorial does a lot of the work.

### The personas and the angles

They target two buyers and write different angles for each.

The first is the skin-and-hair sufferer, women 30-55 who've spent years and money on shampoos, serums, and dermatologist visits and still deal with brittle hair and skin that feels tight after every shower.

Their angles run across awareness levels, from problem-aware ("tired of stepping out of the shower with skin that still feels dry?") to solution-aware ("three-action filtration purifies the water in three stages") to a product-aware comparison against other filters.

The second is the skeptical scrutinizer, buyers who've already seen filtered shower heads from the leader and a dozen Amazon brands, read the Reddit threads, maybe abandoned a cart because it felt expensive, and don't trust what they see online. For them the challenger leans into the skepticism itself. "Filtered shower head but don't want to get scammed? Watch this," then a comparison that sends to the advertorial.

### The full-funnel-in-one-ad structure

This is the part worth stealing. Their longer ads carry a problem-aware buyer all the way to most-aware in a single three-minute video, in four stages:

1. Hook, usually problem-aware. "This shower head changed my life."
2. Mechanism. "Three-action filtration removes chlorine, heavy metals, PFAS, microplastics."
3. Comparison. Position against the alternatives and the cheap knockoffs.
4. Urgency. The offer and why to buy now.

One ad, the whole journey. They also run a green-screen "I need to publicly apologize to anyone who bought this" format that opens like a confession, sells you while you're curious, and only reveals the offer at the end. It works cold and in retargeting because it explains the product before it pitches.

### The four gaps I'd fix

They launched around 830 ads, 810 of them new, 85% video. The volume is there. Here's where I'd push.

First, a third persona they ignore: the wellness optimizer, the biohacker who already filters their drinking water and removes plastic from the kitchen. A hook like "I tested my shower water for microplastics and the results wrecked me" opens a whole new audience, and because it reads as a genuinely different angle it earns a fresh entity ID, which is how you reach new pockets under Andromeda.

Second, authority. They have zero dermatologists, water engineers, or wellness experts in their ad library. A real expert explaining hard-water damage would carry the claims far better than a voiceover.

Third, unaware angles. They barely run them. "Your sunscreen isn't failing, your water is destroying it before it absorbs" reaches people who don't even know the problem exists yet.

Fourth, formats. They repeat a few. A winning angle scales or dies depending on the format it's wrapped in, and podcast ads, POV, Pixar-style animation, and native statics each match different angles. The fastest win we run for clients is taking a proven angle and testing it across formats.

### The actual lesson

To scale you need more winning ads, and you only control two things:

The volume of ads and the win rate.

This brand is doing great in volume.

The next part is quality, ads that don't look like ads and hit a high enough win rate that at least one becomes a top spender every month or two.

That's the whole game. Volume times win rate.

If you're spending $50k+ a month on Meta and want a team that takes your winning angles and scales them across formats, the link is in my bio.
